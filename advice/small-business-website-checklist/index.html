What should a small business website include?

Open your website on a phone and pretend you know nothing about the business. Can you find what it does and how to contact someone without hunting?

A lot of small business websites look finished until you try to use them. The pictures are there, the colors are right, but you can't tell which towns the business serves or where a quote request goes.

Here's a practical checklist to run through before launching or redesigning your site.

The things customers should find quickly

  • What you do: A clear sentence describing the main service. “Quality solutions” doesn't tell a customer whether you repair appliances or build fences.
  • Who you serve and where: Residential or commercial? Houston and Humble, or nationwide remote service? Be specific and accurate.
  • A simple next step: Call, email, request a quote or book a consultation. Pick the action that makes sense for the business.
  • Real contact details: An email address or working form, plus a phone number when appropriate. Check that messages actually arrive.
  • Evidence of your work: Genuine photos, projects, qualifications or reviews. One honest example is better than ten stock images.
  • Useful service pages: Separate important services when customers need different information or have different questions.
  • A readable mobile layout: Text that's easy to read, buttons that aren't difficult to tap, and no content that runs off the screen.
  • Up-to-date information: Correct hours, services, prices where published and working links.

What about Google?

Give each important page a descriptive title and heading. Write naturally about the actual service and service area. If you build fences in Spring, say that where it's helpful. Don't scatter the phrase “best affordable fencing Texas” across every paragraph.

Connect the pages with normal text links, keep the site accessible to search engines, and use Google Search Console to monitor indexing. New pages may take time to appear; a good-looking website isn't an instant top result.

What can wait?

Fancy animations, a complicated booking system, dozens of pages and an oversized gallery can all wait unless customers genuinely need them. Start with a site that is understandable and easy to use. You can improve it as the business grows.

A useful test: Give someone your phone and ask them to find your services, location and contact option in thirty seconds. Don't tell them where to tap. Watch what they do.
