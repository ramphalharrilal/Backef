The Invoice Looks Real. One Changed Detail Could Send Your Money to a Criminal.

A legitimate-looking supplier invoice can hide a fraudulent bank account. Learn how business email compromise works and how to verify changes before paying.

Picture a business that orders from the same supplier every month. The latest invoice arrives for the correct amount, references a real order, and appears to come from a familiar contact. There is just one change: the supplier says it has a new bank account.

The payment goes through. Days later the supplier asks why it has not been paid.

This is a hypothetical example, but the fraud behind it is well documented. It is often called business email compromise, or BEC. The goal is not always to infect a computer. It may simply be to persuade an authorized employee to send money to the wrong place.

How criminals make an ordinary invoice convincing

An attacker may compromise a real mailbox, impersonate a supplier with a lookalike domain, or enter an existing conversation and change the payment instructions. If they can read previous messages, they may learn invoice numbers, delivery dates, staff names and payment habits.

That context makes the false request more convincing. There may be no malicious attachment, suspicious download or link. The instruction itself is the attack.

Why small businesses are attractive targets

A busy owner may handle purchasing, stock, bookkeeping and bank payments without a separate accounting department. A restaurant manager could approve a supplier payment between deliveries; a retailer might pay an invoice while helping customers. Small teams can be efficient, but a missing verification step becomes a single point of failure.

Does checking the sender address solve it?

It helps detect some impersonation attempts, but it is not enough. A compromised genuine mailbox can send from exactly the address you expect. Email authentication standards such as SPF, DKIM and DMARC help reduce some spoofing, but they do not prove that the person sending an otherwise legitimate message is authorized to change bank details.

The most important step: verify independently

When a supplier requests new payment information, phone an established contact using a number already saved in your records. Do not rely only on the contact details attached to the change request. If the instruction arrives by WhatsApp, confirm it through a separate trusted channel. The point is to verify with someone the attacker does not control.

Five controls that work for small teams

  1. Keep an approved supplier list. Record normal contacts and banking details securely, and restrict who can edit them.
  2. Use a standard change procedure. Require independent confirmation before changing payment destination, regardless of how urgent the message sounds.
  3. Separate approval when practical. Have a second person check unusually large transfers or new beneficiaries.
  4. Protect your email. Use unique passwords and multifactor authentication; review unfamiliar login sessions and unexpected forwarding rules.
  5. Make verification acceptable. Employees should know they can slow down to confirm an instruction, even if it appears to come from the owner.

What if the money has already been sent?

Contact the bank or payment provider immediately, identify the transfer as potentially fraudulent, and ask about recall or freezing options. Recovery is never guaranteed. Keep the original messages, invoices and transaction records, report the incident to the appropriate authorities, and investigate whether a company or supplier email account was compromised.

The bigger lesson

Firewalls, antivirus tools and account security remain essential. But a perfectly secure computer cannot tell whether a new bank account actually belongs to your supplier.

When banking instructions change, verify the change through a channel you already trust before moving money. It is a small business process with potentially enormous value.
