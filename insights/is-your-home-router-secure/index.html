Your Wi-Fi Works Fine. So How Would You Know if Your Router Was Hacked?

Your router can keep the internet working while its settings are unsafe. Learn what the April 2026 DNS hijacking operation revealed and which settings to check.

Most households have a simple relationship with their internet router: someone installs it, connects the phones and televisions, and leaves it alone until the internet stops working. A good connection feels like proof everything is fine. Unfortunately, the router's security and the speed of your Wi-Fi are two very different things.

Your router is the connection point between your home and the wider internet. It handles traffic, assigns addresses, often provides basic firewall features, and may tell your devices which DNS service to use. A device can perform all those tasks while running outdated software or using settings that an owner never intended.

A warning from a real investigation

On April 7, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice and FBI announced an operation disrupting a network of home and small-office routers compromised by a Russian military intelligence unit. Investigators said the attackers exploited vulnerabilities in routers and altered DNS settings to facilitate attacks against selected targets. This was not a claim that every home was being watched. It was evidence that ordinary networking equipment could become part of sophisticated operations without obviously failing.

What DNS hijacking means

The Domain Name System (DNS) helps turn a website name into the numerical address your device uses to make a connection. A router may tell devices which DNS resolver to consult. If an attacker controls that setting, they may be able to direct certain name lookups toward malicious infrastructure.

HTTPS and certificate checks can make interception harder, but they do not make unexpected DNS changes harmless. A malicious response may be combined with convincing imitation pages, attempts to collect credentials, or other attack techniques. Never casually ignore a browser's certificate warning.

Your Wi-Fi password is only one layer

A strong wireless password helps keep unauthorized nearby devices off your network. It does not repair a vulnerability in your router's firmware. It is also different from the administrator password used to change router settings. Attackers exploiting internet-facing services may not need your Wi-Fi password at all.

Six checks worth making this week

  1. Find the make and model. Check the label or router app, then look on the manufacturer's official support site for firmware updates and security notices.
  2. Confirm support has not ended. If a router no longer gets security updates, plan to replace it rather than assume it is safe because it still works.
  3. Protect the administrator login. Change default credentials and use a unique password, separate from the Wi-Fi password.
  4. Review remote administration. Unless it is required, disable the ability to administer the router from outside your home network.
  5. Use suitable Wi-Fi encryption. Choose WPA3 Personal where supported; WPA2 Personal with AES is an appropriate alternative for compatible older devices. Avoid WEP.
  6. Look at connected devices and DNS settings. Check for unknown connections and unexpected DNS resolvers. An unfamiliar device label alone is not proof of compromise, but it merits investigation.

And your smart devices?

Cameras, TVs, printers and smart appliances may use the same network as computers containing important personal information. A properly isolated guest or smart-device network can limit unnecessary access between them. It will not solve weak camera-account passwords or outdated device software, so those require attention too.

If something looks wrong

Document unusual changes and consult the router manufacturer's guidance or a qualified technician. Updating firmware and correcting settings may be required; in some circumstances a factory reset or replacement may be necessary. A reset alone cannot permanently solve an unpatched vulnerability.

The lesson: a reliable connection tells you that your router is doing its job, not that it is doing it securely. Check the software and settings behind the signal.
