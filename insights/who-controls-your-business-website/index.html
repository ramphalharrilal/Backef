You Paid for Your Website. But Who Actually Controls It?

Your website can work perfectly while your domain, hosting or business accounts are controlled by someone else. Learn the ownership and access checks every owner should make.

A business can pay for its website, use a company email address every day and run a successful Facebook page while having surprisingly little control over the accounts behind them.

It often begins innocently. An employee registers the domain. A web designer signs up for hosting. A friend connects the email. Someone creates the Facebook page using a personal account. Years later everything still works, so nobody looks into who controls what.

The problem becomes visible when someone leaves, an account is locked, an administrator is unavailable or a critical change must happen quickly.

Paying for a website is not the same as controlling every account

Several systems usually keep a small business online:

  • Domain registrar: manages registration of the business's web address.
  • DNS provider: directs website and email traffic to the correct services.
  • Web host: delivers the files or applications that make the site work.
  • Email provider: controls company mailboxes and their recovery settings.
  • Website administrator accounts: manage content, integrations and sometimes customer data.
  • Third-party platforms: handle booking, payments, analytics or social media.

These may have separate invoices, permissions and recovery methods. Access to the website editor does not necessarily provide authority over the domain or email.

Why domain control matters so much

Your domain is more than an address printed on a business card. It may also support every business email address and multiple systems that verify domain ownership. If its renewal fails, its records are changed without authorization or its account becomes inaccessible, the disruption can affect far more than the website.

Find out who the registrar is, which business-authorized account controls the registration, who can change DNS settings and when renewal is due. Enable appropriate account security and maintain accurate recovery information.

When one person holds all the keys

An external administrator may manage everything efficiently for years. That is not automatically a problem, and skilled contractors are valuable. But a business needs a plan for what happens when that person is unavailable or the relationship ends.

Shared administrator passwords are particularly problematic. They make changes harder to attribute, complicate offboarding, and do not necessarily invalidate existing sessions or connected application tokens when the password changes.

Individual accounts, appropriate access levels, multifactor authentication and documented recovery processes are healthier arrangements. Under the principle of least privilege, someone changing opening hours does not need permission to transfer the domain.

Six questions to ask before there is an emergency

  1. Who controls the domain? Can the business renew it and prove account authority without relying on a former employee?
  2. Who can change DNS and hosting? Can you identify every authorized administrator?
  3. Who controls business email? Are recovery methods current and business-authorized?
  4. Are backups available? What is backed up, who can retrieve it, and has restoration been tested?
  5. Can your services move? Do you know how to transfer the domain and migrate the website if needed?
  6. Who can access social platforms? Have you removed old administrators from business pages and advertising accounts?

A simple digital ownership inventory

Create a protected record listing each important service, its provider, purpose, designated owner, administrators, renewal date and recovery process. Do not store passwords in a plain spreadsheet. Use an appropriately secured password manager or credential system.

Then consider this scenario: your main website administrator is unavailable tomorrow. Could you regain access to your website, domain, email and customer information? If the answer is uncertain, you have identified a business continuity risk worth fixing now.

Security means retaining control

A company might have excellent website design, secure hosting and modern cloud software, but still be vulnerable if essential accounts are tied to one person's private email or nobody knows how to recover access.

Digital ownership is not about doing all the technical work yourself. It is about knowing who has authority, ensuring the business can recover access, and keeping critical services under accountable control.
